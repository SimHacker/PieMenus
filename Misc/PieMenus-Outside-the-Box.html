What is a Pie Menu?

A pie menu is a user interface technique for selection based on direction. A goal of pie menus is to provide a smooth, reliable gestural style of interaction for novices and experts.

What's wrong with traditional linear menus?

Traditional linear "pull down" menus require you to always move the cursor in the same direction (down), but for a different distance to select each item, so all items are hard to select (because of their small target size), and items further down on the menu take even longer to select (because of their greater distance).

Fitts' Law

Fitts' Law predicts the larger and closer a target, the less time it takes to select.


D is target distance, and W is target width.
From the equation, we see a speed-accuracy tradeoff associated with pointing, whereby targets that are smaller and/or further away require more time to acquire.
Therefore, math = fun.

Pie menus exploit Fitts' Law, because they optimize for both large target size and small target distance, by placing each item adjacent to the cursor, but in a different direction.

Directional Nature

And because of their directional nature, it's easy to select an item from a pie menu without looking at the screen, and the further out you move, the more reliable control you have over the direction (by increasing "leverage").

But linear menus make you move the mouse a certain distance, which requires much more visual attention and hand-eye feedback, and prevents you from reliably "mousing ahead", spoiling the possibility of quick gestures.

Mouse Gestures and Muscle Memory

Once you learn the pie menus direction with "muscle memory", you can quickly and reliably "mouse ahead" to select items, without looking at the screen -- so pie menus support self revealing, unambiguous, predictable, gestural interaction.

Unambiguity, Precision and Error Correction

All pie menu gestures are valid, unambiguous selections, directly defined in a way that the user can easily understand. The selection is defined purely by the direction (not the path or distance) between the down and up events. The further out you move, the better control you have over the direction. You can always correct the selection after making a mistake or changing your mind.

Compare pie menus to general gesture and handwriting recognition: Pie menus fully saturate "gesture space", while most handwriting gestures are syntax errors. Where is the boundary between an "S" and a "5"?

Problems, Approaches and Solutions

Limited Number of Items

Pie menus are harder to use with too many and odd numbers of items. Their ease of use and memorability depends heavily on how many items they have, and it's a complex non-linear relationship. There are many ways to deal with too many and odd numbers of items in different contexts.

Ideal Numbers of Items

Four is the most ideal number of items, which is very reliable, and works well with small touch screens and big fingers. Eight is the idea number if the screen is large enough and the input device is precise enough. Twelve is getting crowded, but works well because of its association with the clock and zodiac. It's very desirable to have an even number of items on the menu, to keep it synmetrical, even if it means adding an inactive blank dummy item.

Nested Sub-Menus

Pie menu items can invoke both pie and linear nested sub-menus. Nesting large numbers of items works well if you can subdivide them into logically related groups, there are not too many of them, and they are not dynamic. But it's easy to get carried away and design trees of menus that are too deep or too wide.

Scrolling and Paging

Scrolling and paging pie menus reveal more items as you wind around or page up and down a spiral or stack of pies. They can remember your place in the sequence, like a scrollbar does in a drop-down list. It's best that the directions of the items not change as you scroll, because that would disorient and defeat muscle memory.

Pull-Out Pie Menus

Pull-out pie menus can assign several items to each direction, which you select between by pulling out from the menu center. This technique sacrifices the user's ability to trade off distance for precision, but works well for a small number of related items (small/medium/large), or one parameterized item (font/size).

Ringed Pie Menus

Ringed pie menus have several layers of items. Each concentric layer may have a different number of items, so outer rings typically have more items than inner rings (like an atom). This technique also sacrifices the user's ability to trade off distance for precision, but it enables you to put a lot of items on one menu, in priority order. It does look rather cluttered, unless you can get away with using small squareish icons instead of elongated text.

    

Hybrid Linear/Pie Menus

Hybrid pie/linear menus can combine items in the circular pie format with items in linear format in any direction (pull down/up/right/left). The linear items are selected by pointing at their label rectangle. The hybrid menu can limit the number of pie menu items (to 8 by default), and place overflow items in linear rows or columns.

User Editable Menus

User editable pie menus solve the problem elegently by enabling each users to define their own menus of comonly used commands, according to their own particular work flow. Alias|Wavefront enables 3D artists to define their own "marking menus" for the various tasks they work on and tools they use. User editable pie menus can use dialogs, text, xml, or direct manipulation to enable users to construct their own menu structures. The iLoci application lets you define networks of rooms that you navigate with pie gestures.
