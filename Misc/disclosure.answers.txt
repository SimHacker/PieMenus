		       DISCLOSING AN INVENTION


The business of a university is the creation and dissemination of
knowledge. Making an invention and putting it to use in the service of
the public is a thoroughly valid mode of accomplishing this objective.

Accordingly, the University encourages the inventive process and
within the limits of financial practicality, can often provide advice
and assistance in bringing inventions to the point of public use. The
University's Patent Policy sets forth the University's position in
detail. A copy may be obtained on request to the Technology Liaison
Office (TLO).

In the sense used here, an "invention" has a presumed commercial use
and value. From this certain caveats follow as a consequence:

1. Disclose first, publish later.  Disclosing your invention by no
means proscribes publication; on the other hand, premature publication
can have both legal and tactical effects on the University's efforts
to commercialize it.

2. Disclose your idea verbally to the TLO as soon as the invention is
clearly conceptualized.  It is not wise to wait for reduction to
practice, and the TLO can assist you in determining the timing for
completing the disclosure form.

3. Leave procedural questions to the specialists.  For example,
federal funding does not usually impede commercialization. What
sometimes constitutes a patentable invention is a complex legal
question.

If you think you have made an invention, but you are not sure, consult
the TLO before completing the formal disclosure form. We will find out
for you.

If you think, but are uncertain, that your invention has commercial
merit, say so. Perhaps we can encourage you, or save a lot of wasted
effort.

Since the majority, if not all of the disclosures we receive are in
the very early stages, it is critical that we have your total
cooperation and assistance during the patenting and marketing process
of your invention. This is very important, and by placement of your
signature on the Disclosure of Invention Form, we will accept the
fact that you are in agreement, and will work with us to the best of
your ability.

The form attached is offered as a guide to help in getting your
invention down in writing.

	Pies: Implementation and Evaluation of Circular Menus
	  By Don Hopkins, Jack Callahan, and Mark Weiser
	  (Paper in preparation. Draft available from authors.)

	  Introduces Pie Menus, a new type of menu whose choices are
	  positioned a circle around the cursor, like the slices of a
	  pie.  They are compared and contrasted with pull down menus,
	  and ideas about their usage and applications are discussed.

	A Comparitive Ananysis of Pie Menu Performance
	  By Jack Callahan, Don Hopkins, Mark Weiser, and Ben Schneiderman
	  (Paper in preparation. Draft available from authors.)

	  Details the design, procedures, and results of an experiment
	  comparing Pie Menus with pull down menus. The experiment
	  compared the selection time and error rate for choices from
	  Pie and pull down menus of eight items. Menus tasks were
	  classified by the type of their selections, as Pie type
	  tasks, linear type tasks, and unclassified type tasks.  Pie
	  menus were shown to be significantly faster and more
	  reliable than pull down menus for all three types of tasks.

   State all of the alternate forms envisioned to be within the full

Pie menus may be used with many types of input devices, including:

    Mouse, track ball, mole (foot mouse), Left/right up/down Atari
    joystick, arrow keys on key pad, touch pad, touch screen, graphics
    tablet, joystick, lightpen, eye motion sensors.

Pie Menus input two dimensional data (direction, distance). Each
dimension can be continuous or discrete.  There are many ways in which
the data may be interpreted. A Pie Menu may be used as a metaphore for
many types of input devices:

    Light switch (2 slices), compass (8, or continuous direction),
    clock hands (setting the time), sliders (direction chooses between
    several, and distance sets it), knobs (continuous direction), 2
    dimensional vector input, 3 dimensional unit vector input (surface
    orientation, 3-d rotation).

5. Novel Features.

   a. Specify the novel features of your invention. How does the
invention differ from present technology?

The novel feature of Pie Menus is that selection is based on the
direction specified by the user, allowing for the menu items to be
equidistant from and near to the starting location, thus equally easy
to choose. So the distance of movement required to make a selection is
constant.  This definition of selection allows for the distance,
independant of the direction, to be used a further argument to the
menu selection. The greater the distance, the more precision the user
has in specifying the direction. 

Selection from linear menus is based on the rectangular area of the
screen pointed to by the user. To point to each different area, the
user must move a different distance, usually in the same direction.
The distance of movement required to make a selection depends on the
selection, while the direction of movement is constant.


   b. What is the deficiency in the present technology which your
invention improves upon, or the limitations it overcomes?

Pie menus make selection faster and more reliable. Visual feedback is
not required when the user is familiar with a Pie menu, allowing
selections to be made reliably without looking at the menu.  The
spatial relationships between the selections in a pie menu may be
exploited to make the selection directions intuitive and easy to
remember. The directions of the choices in a Pie menu are much easier
to learn and remember than the order of the choices in a linear menu.

Because it does not effect the selection made from the menu, the
distance of movement is a natural way of providing input to the menu
selection.
